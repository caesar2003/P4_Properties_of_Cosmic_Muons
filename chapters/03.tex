\section{Panel efficiency}
The efficiency of the panels can be calculated in the following way: All events in which e.g. scintillator 1 and 3 were triggered would suggest that scintillator 2 should have been triggered as well. This works fine for scintillators 2 and 3. For scintillators 1 and 4 the method has to be adjusted a bit. For e.g. scintillator 1 all events are taken into account in which at least two of the other scintillators were triggered. The problem is that this could have been triggered by a muon which is approaching at a slight angle in such a way that in never passed through scintillator 1. For scintillator 4 the decay of the muon is another effect which slightly changes the efficiency. These effects are not taken into account in this experiment. 

First the data is inspected to make sure all panels and the trigger work properly.

As seen in \autoref{fig:even_rate_eff} the event rate is approximately constant which is good.

The pulse height spectrum of every individual panels is shown in \autoref{fig:pulse_heights}. Since they all have approximately the same peak height they all seem to work fine. Note that the peaks at zero for Panels B - D are caused by muons which were detected by panel A (and therefore a event was triggered) but decayed since then or passed at such an angle through the detector that the other panels were never triggered.

\section{Lifetime}
The lifetime measurement for works the following way:
As soon as a muon was detected in scintillator 1 and 2 but not in scintillator 3  and 4 it can be assumed that the muon decayed inside the copper plate. This event starts the beginning of a lifetime measurement. A event where scintillator 2 or 2 and 1 but not 3 or 3 and 4 (for a positron which is emitted upwards) or 3 or 3 and 4 but not 2 or 2 and 1 (for a positron which is emitted downwards) are triggered marks the end of a lifetime measurement. A lifetime measurement signal is shown in \autoref{fig:lifetime_signal}. The second peak is a signal from scintillator 3 and 4 which means that the positron was emitted downwards.

\end{figure}

\section{Procedure for Detecting Muon Spin}
\textit{The following section is based on the provided Jupyter notebook for the muon lifetime experiment \cite{Jupyter}.}
As an alternative to the simple exponential decay law, a parameterization of the number of decays detected in the upper detector at time \( t \), which includes a small additional oscillating component (see experiment instructions in the Blue Book) is used:

\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{pdf}_{\mathrm{signal}}(t) = 
  K \cdot e^{-t/\tau} \cdot \left[1 + \bar{A} \cdot \cos(\omega t + \delta) \right]
\end{equation}

To use this probability density in a fit, it must be normalized over the sensitive time interval \( t \in [a, b] \). The required normalization constant \( K \) is computed via:

{\scriptsize
\begin{align}
  K^{-1}
  = \tau \Bigg( 
    \frac{ \bar{A} \Big[ 
      e^{-a/\tau} \left( \cos(a \omega + \delta) - \tau \omega \sin(a \omega + \delta) \right) 
      + e^{-b/\tau} \left( \tau \omega \sin(b \omega + \delta) - \cos(b \omega + \delta) \right)
    \Big] }{ \tau^2 \omega^2 + 1 }
  + e^{-a/\tau} - e^{-b/\tau}
  \Bigg)
\end{align}
}




\subsection*{Background Component}

The background is modeled as a constant probability density in the same time interval:

\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{pdf}_{\mathrm{bkg}}(t) = \frac{1}{b - a}
\end{equation}

With a background fraction \( f \), the total probability density function becomes:

\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{pdf}(t; \tau, f, \bar{A}, \omega, \delta, a, b) = 
  (1 - f) \cdot \mathrm{pdf}_{\mathrm{signal}}(t) + 
  f \cdot \mathrm{pdf}_{\mathrm{bkg}}(t)
\end{equation}

\subsection*{Phase Shift for Lower Detector}

For decays detected in the lower detector, located below the copper absorber, the modulation is phase-shifted by \( 180^\circ \). This effect can be modeled by appropriately adjusting the phase parameter \( \delta \). With this adjustment, the same functional form can be reused for both detectors.

\subsection*{Adjustments of the pure exponential model ("Null Hypothesis") and the model with spin modulation}

Now the distribution of the muon decay times are analysed by fitting two different models to the measured lifetime data. First a pure exponential decay model is applied, representing the null hypothesis with no spin precession. This yields initial estimates for the muon lifetime $\tau$ and the background fraction $f_\mathrm{bg}$. These fits are on the left hand side of \autoref{fig:histfit1}  and \autoref{fig:histfit2}. In a second step, a refined model including spin modulation is fitted to the data, with $\tau$ and $f_\mathrm{bg}$ fixed values from the first step. This allows the extraction of the asymmetry parameter $\bar{A}$. These fits are on the right hand side of \autoref{fig:histfit1} and \autoref{fig:histfit2}.

\subsection*{Statistical evaluation and determination of the signal significance}

Finally the statistical significance of the two different fits, one a pure exponential (null hypothesis), the other one additionally considering the muon precession (alternative precession), is being determined. 

The comparison is based on the values of the negative log-likelihood (NLL) from both fits. The test statistic is the difference of these values, defined as

\begin{equation}
    z^2 = -2 \cdot \Delta \ln \mathcal{L} = -2 \cdot (\mathrm{NLL}_{\text{mod}} - \mathrm{NLL}_{\text{non-mod}}),
\end{equation}

where a higher $z^2$ value indicates a stronger preference for the modulation model. The square root $z$ corresponds to the significance of the observed modulation in terms of standard deviations.


With $z$, the corresponding $p$-value is calculated using the cumulative distribution function $\Phi$ of the standard normal distribution:

\begin{equation}
    p = 1 - 2\,(1 - \Phi(z)), \quad z \ge 0.
\end{equation}

The $p$-value is a measure for the probability that the observed deviation from the null hypothesis is purely due to statistical fluctuations. Small $p$-values therefore indicate statistically significant signals.

This evaluation helps decide whether the oscillation seen in the data is meaningful.\\

For both the upper and lower detectors, the fits including modulation yield significantly better agreement with the data compared to the pure exponential model. The statistical results are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Upper Detector (B)}: $z = 3.52 \Rightarrow p = 0.02195\%$
    \item \textbf{Lower Detector (C)}:  $z = 3.29 \Rightarrow p = 0.04925\%$
\end{itemize}

In both cases, the $p$-values are well below the common threshold of 5\%, allowing us to reject the null hypothesis. This provides strong evidence for the presence of a modulation in the muon decay time distribution, consistent with spin precession.
